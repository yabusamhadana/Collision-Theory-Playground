Study guide

Reaction mechanisms

  • A mechanism is a sequence of elementary reactions that add up to the overall reaction.
  • An elementary reaction is a single chemical step with one transition state. Its rate scales with each reactant concentration raised to its stoichiometric coefficient. This holds for elementary steps only, not for overall reactions.
  • Example: O₃ + O → 2O₂ occurs as O₃ + Cl → O₂ + ClO, then O + ClO → O₂ + Cl.
  • An intermediate is formed in an earlier step and consumed in a later one (ClO).
  • A catalyst is consumed early and re-formed at the end (Cl), so it is absent from the overall equation.

Molecularity

  • Unimolecular: one reactant particle, A → products, rate = k[A]. Examples: isomerization, bond breaking (N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂), radioactive decay.
  • Bimolecular: exactly two reactant particles collide with one transition state. A + B → products has rate = k[A][B]; 2A → products has rate = k[A]². Example: bond formation, 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄.
  • Termolecular: exactly three reactant particles. These steps are very slow, so A + B + C → products is unlikely as one step. Expect a multistep path, such as A + B → D and then D + C → products.

Factors that affect reaction rate

  • To change a rate, change the collision energy or the collision frequency.
  • Temperature: a higher T raises the average kinetic energy of collisions and gives a larger rate constant (Arrhenius equation, k = Ae^(−Ea/RT)).
  • Concentration: higher concentration means more frequent collisions, which usually means a faster reaction. A zero-order reaction is the exception, since its rate does not depend on concentration.
  • Phase: reactants in different phases can only collide at phase interfaces.
  • Catalyst: gives a mechanism with a lower activation energy, and is not consumed overall.
  • Ionization energy trend: when losing an electron controls the rate (for example, alkali metals reacting), a smaller first ionization energy usually goes with a smaller Ea and a faster reaction. This is a trend, not a rule.

Rate laws and reaction order

  • For aA + bB → dD + eE: rate = k[A]^m[B]^n. The exponents m and n are the reaction orders, and the total order is m + n.
  • Orders show how each concentration affects the rate. They are found by experiment and need not equal the stoichiometric coefficients.
  • To determine a rate law, find the orders and the rate constant. Temperature, concentration and time are easy to measure.

Rate-determining step

  • The rate-determining step is the slowest step in a mechanism, and the overall rate is approximately the rate of that step.
  • Example: 2I⁻ + H₂O₂ + 2H⁺ → I₂ + 2H₂O must be multistep, because its orders do not match its coefficients. The observed rate is k[I⁻][H₂O₂].
  • Equilibrium approximation: if the slow step is not the first step, the concentration of the intermediate is set by the fast step before it, through the ratio of its forward and reverse rate constants.

Catalysis and enzymes

  • Homogeneous catalyst: same phase as the reactants, so its concentration matters.
  • Heterogeneous catalyst: different phase from the reactants, so its available surface area matters.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins. The reactant an enzyme binds is the substrate, and the part that interacts with it is the active site.
  • Denaturation is the loss of an enzyme’s quaternary, tertiary and secondary structure. Inhibitors decrease an enzyme’s catalytic efficiency.

Energetics

  • Exergonic: G of products is less than G of reactants, and the reaction is spontaneous.
  • Endergonic: G of products is greater than G of reactants, and the reaction is nonspontaneous.
  • Coupled reactions exchange energy, so an exergonic reaction can drive an endergonic one.
  • Spontaneous does not mean fast. Free energy says whether a reaction can occur, and kinetics says how quickly.
